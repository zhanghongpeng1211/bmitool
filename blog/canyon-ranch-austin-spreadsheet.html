I Spent a Week at Canyon Ranch Austin. I Brought a Spreadsheet.

May 10, 2026 · 1300 words · By James "Jamie" Whitfield
Advertisement

Canyon Ranch Austin opened its doors in March 2026. You’ve probably seen the billboards on 35: “The Ultimate Wellness Experience. Opening Soon.” The property is near Lake Austin, tucked into the hills off 2222, about 15 minutes from my apartment in East Austin but mentally a different planet. Valet parking. Cold lavender-scented towels upon arrival. A check-in desk that offered me a “wellness shot” (ginger, turmeric, something green). I was not the target demographic. The target demographic is “people who can spend $12,000 on a week of wellness without flinching.” I am “people who compare H-E-B brand vs name brand per-ounce pricing and buy the store brand.”

But I got a media pass for five days in exchange for honest data — no editorial control from Canyon Ranch, just a written agreement that I would share my findings publicly. They wanted the publicity. I wanted the DEXA scan. So I packed my laptop, my measuring tape, my wearable, and my spreadsheet templates, and drove out there on a Sunday afternoon.

Day 1: Arrival and biometrics. Upon check-in, they drew blood (fasting lipid panel, glucose, insulin, HbA1c, vitamin D, thyroid panel), did a DEXA scan (whole body and regional), a VO2 max test on a treadmill (with a mask, the whole thing), and a resting metabolic rate (RMR) measurement via indirect calorimetry (you breathe into a tube for 20 minutes while lying down). I’ve had DEXA before at a mobile clinic, but never with this level of prep. The technician walked me through my results in a private consultation room.

My DEXA results: 18.2% body fat (total), with regional breakdown: 14.3% in arms, 19.8% in trunk, 16.7% in legs. My lean mass: 141.2 lbs. Bone density: Z-score +0.4 (normal). My Navy method estimate that morning (done at home before I left — waist 34.8 in, neck 16.0 in, height 70 in) gave me 19.1% body fat. Off by 0.9 percentage points. That’s within the margin of error of the Navy method (reported as ±3% in the original 1985 study and ±2% in a 2018 validation). Not bad for a $0 method vs a $300 scan.

RMR measured: 1,680 calories/day. My online calculator (Mifflin-St Jeor, which I have on bmitool.org) gave me 1,650. Within 2%. I felt weirdly validated. The dietitian said my RMR was “exactly where we’d expect for your lean mass” — about 11.9 cal per lb of lean mass, which is standard. Then she told me that most of their guests have RMRs 10-20% below predicted because of chronic dieting and muscle loss. That was sobering.

Day 2: Movement. They have a “functional fitness” lab with force plates embedded in the floor and a 12-camera motion capture system. I did a deadlift (135 lbs, not impressive) and watched my bar path on a screen in real time. The coach pointed out that I shift weight to my left side at the top of the lift — likely from an old right ankle sprain (I rolled it playing pickup basketball at Austin Rec Center four years ago). I’ve deadlifted for years and never noticed. The tech was overkill, but the insight was real. He gave me a single cue: “squeeze your right glute before you pull.” It fixed the shift immediately.

 Day 3: Food. The dining philosophy is “plant-forward, not plant-only.” Breakfast buffet (included in the package): chia pudding with hemp seeds and berries, two scrambled eggs, half an avocado. I measured everything with my pocket scale (yes, I brought it). 340 cal, 12g protein, 9g fiber. Lunch: roasted cauliflower steak over lentil salad with tahini dressing. 520 cal, 22g protein. Dinner: salmon with farro and broccolini. 610 cal, 38g protein. Total day: about 1,800 calories. By day three I was hungry. Not starving, but noticeably lower energy than usual. My Garmin showed active calories burned of 2,400 (I walked a lot around the property — 15,000 steps — plus the morning strength session). Deficit of 600 calories. That’s fine for a week — I lost about 1.2 lbs by the end. For a lifetime? No. The dietitian agreed: “We feed people for healing here, not for maintenance. You’ll go back to normal portions when you leave.” Day 4: The weird stuff. Canyon Ranch offers Ayurvedic consultations, sound baths, acupuncture, reiki, and something called “vibrational sound healing with tuning forks.” I tried the sound bath (again, after my SXSW experience) and this time actually tried to engage instead of rolling my eyes. Lying on a mat in a dim room, 40 minutes of crystal bowls and a gong. My heart rate dropped from 72 to 61. My HRV (heart rate variability, measured by my Garmin) went from 35ms to 52ms during the session. That’s a real physiological response — a 2024 study in *Frontiers in Psychology* (n=60, randomized controlled trial) found that sound meditation reduced cortisol by 23% and increased HRV by 18% compared to silent rest. I’m not converting to woo. But I’m not dismissing it either. I’ll probably do a 10-minute version at home using a free app. I will not be buying $500 crystal bowls.

 Day 5: The bill. They didn’t charge me (media pass), but I asked to see the standard package pricing. The 5-night “Wellness Immersion” includes: all meals, all assessments (DEXA, VO2 max, RMR, blood work), two “integrative health coaching” sessions, two fitness classes per day, and unlimited access to the spa facilities (sauna, steam room, cold plunge, hot tub). Cost: $9,800 plus tax. For 5 nights. That’s more than my monthly mortgage on my East Austin bungalow (which I bought in 2019 before prices went insane). For comparison, a DEXA scan at a mobile clinic in Austin costs $150. A VO2 max test at UT Austin’s Human Performance Lab is $200. A full lipid panel through a direct-to-consumer lab is $100. The coaching and classes are valuable, but you could assemble the same services a la carte for maybe $2,000-3,000. The data I brought home: Five days of perfect sleep (averaged 7h52m vs my usual 6h20m at home — the blackout curtains and soundproofing were incredible). Five days of 16,000+ steps (vs my usual 8,000). Five days of no alcohol (vs my usual 3-5 drinks per week, mostly craft beer at Zilker Brewing or Lazarus). Five days of no caffeine after noon (Canyon Ranch bans coffee after 2 PM). My RHR dropped from 68 to 62. My HRV went from 35ms to 52ms. My blood pressure went from 118/76 to 108/70. Those changes lasted about two weeks after I left, then slowly faded as I returned to my normal habits. The honest take: Canyon Ranch is not worth $10k for most people. But the *protocol* — good sleep (consistent timing, dark room, cool temperature), high daily steps (15k+), whole foods (minimally processed, high fiber, moderate protein), stress reduction (10-20 min of quiet time daily), and no alcohol — is worth everything. You can do 80% of that at home for free. The remaining 20% (DEXA, VO2 max, motion capture, blood work) is fun and informative but not life-changing. I learned that my RMR is normal, my body fat is in the fitness range, and my deadlift form has a left-side bias. Those are useful data points. They didn’t change my behavior as much as the sleep and walking protocol did.

I left with a renewed commitment to sleep and daily steps. I bought blackout curtains from H-E-B (the kind that actually block light, $35). I started setting a 10 PM phone alarm to remind myself to get ready for bed. I’ve maintained about 12,000 steps per day (up from 8,000). My RHR is now 64, down from 68. I did not buy the $150 sound bath app. And I’m still not doing cold plunges in Lake Austin.

— Jamie
